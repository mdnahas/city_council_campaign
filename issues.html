Austin Board of Realtors (ABoR)

2026-07-20 · Questionnaire

Q: Why are you running for this office?

The City government has a problem with finances, I have the skills to help, and no one else on City Council or running for it focuses on this issue.

One of our city's financial problems is under-funding the pension funds by $3.1 billion. That costs us $219 million per year or roughly 1/6th of the City's general fund, and the current plan is to borrow more for the next 5 years. Another problem is unwise large spending. Council chose light rail, which is twice as expensive as bus-rapid-transit per passenger-mile for only slightly faster service. Council spent $1+ billion on a new convention center based on a poorly argued financial prospectus.

I have worked in finance and studied economics. I'm well off and would rather spend my time swimming in Barton Springs and doing other enjoyable things. But I love Austin and want to see its government functioning well for my niece and nephews. Because no one else was prioritizing these financial issues, I decided to run for City Council.

Q: What are your top three priority issues?

  • Keep making it easier to build housing. (This lowers rents, lowers homelessness, and lowers the prices of starter homes.)
  • A real plan for pension underfunding. (This will keep services high and taxes low long-term.)
  • Stopping wasteful spending. (This will keep services high and taxes low long-term.)

Q: Briefly describe any current or previous community engagement or service you have done.

For about 8 years, I have worked to lower rents in Austin with AURA, a local non-profit. This included doing data analysis, writing economic reports, and serving on its Board for a term. My focus was on shrinking the minimum lot size and increasing the number of units-per-acre. AURA was instrumental in passing HOMES1 and HOMES2, which did just that. (And rents went down!)

For about 3 years, I've served on the Economic Prosperity Commission. I was Vice Chair and then Chair. I had the most recommendations passed as a Member in my time. I did not seek re-election as Chair so that I would have time to study Austin's pension funds. I spent 100 hours studying the issue, wrote a report on it, and got 2 recommendations passed on the subject.

For about 2 years, I served on the Board of Community Housing Expansion of Austin (CHEA). It runs 2 housing co-operatives in Austin, including some of the lowest-cost housing available. I think it was excellent preparation for City Council because it had many crises and CHEA had its own rules and each house had its own as well.

I've also written a report with economic recommendations for the Mayor, fund-raised to fight gerrymandering, built houses with Habitat, helped clean up parks, mapped city trails with GPS, donated to local causes, and more.

Q: How will you protect private property rights for property owners while advancing citywide housing goals?

I think the good model to imitate is Houston's minimum lot size change. Their City Council dramatically shrank the minimum lot size for the city, but allowed residents to keep the minimum lot size on their block. Residents could apply to create "Special Minimum Lot Size Blocks" that last for 40 years. If the application was accepted, those residents kept their minimum lot size and the City government would enforce it. By allowing and protecting exceptions, Houston passed one of the most dramatic and effective housing policies in America.

I will pair strong pro-housing changes with a legal defense fund. Since many of our property rights are civil, not criminal, enforcing them is a big expense for residents in time, effort, and money. The City should establish a legal defense fund for property owners against neighbors (especially well-financed, well-motivated developers) who violate agreements and deed restrictions. I would only allow the defense fund to work on agreements and deed restrictions that [1] were adopted after the defense fund was created and [2] have a limited time span. (The deed restrictions that last forever are currently harming our city and are damned hard to get rid of. I don't want to create more of those.)

Q: What is your assessment of HOME’s early impacts, and what refinements (if any) would you support?

HOME Phase 1 passed in Dec. 2023 and HOME Phase 2 passed in May 2024. As an urban economist, the metric I use to evaluate housing policy is the All-Transactions House Price Index for Austin-Round Rock-Georgetown, TX (MSA), when adjusted for inflation. (https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/ATNHPIUS12420Q) This metric estimates the price change for all existing houses. When adjusted for inflation, the price of an existing house in the Austin metro fell 5.7% between Dec. 2023 and Feb. 2026. That's success to me.

I don't think HOME went far enough in allowing more units-per-acre and getting rid of the minimum lot size. The minimum lot size still forces households to spend more money on land than they want to. I know this because, in Mueller, which has fewer restrictions, smaller lots are sold. The cheapest lot on Zillow in District 1 within 3 miles of downtown is for sale at $175,000 per unit. At current mortgage rates, that's $890 per month just for land. That doesn't include the house or maintenance or any other housing expense. Philadelphia allows lots of 800 sqft, which is less than half Austin's current minimum lot size. If our minimum lot size were 800 sqft, the price would fall to $444 per month for land. I think that's a more reasonable price and that's the direction Austin should go.

Q: How should housing and land use policy align with transit and infrastructure investments to support growth?

People choose housing with transportation in mind. Those are a single decision, not separate ones. So, we cannot ignore transportation when it comes to housing. Cars are useful, but traffic congestion already costs the metro area $807 million per year. We need to look at other transportation as well as improve car infrastructure.

The best alternative transportation infrastructure is the one already being heavily used: sidewalks. We need to encourage housing within downtown by widening sidewalks where necessary and freeing up zoning. We need to build more sidewalks - the city is horribly behind - focusing first near transit stations, dangerous areas, and where "desire paths" exist.

We should create bike lanes where they are used. I do not see many bikes in the bike lane of the 183 frontage road near me. I do see a lot of bikes downtown on 3rd Street and on the Pfluger Bike Bridge. I would need to do more research on the subject, but a social media post about Amsterdam said their bike-to-work radius was 3 miles with an e-bike. I expect the total benefit minus cost of providing protected bike lanes to exceed that of parked cars inside that radius. If so, we should quickly build protected bike lanes inside 3 miles and remove zoning restrictions nearby. Safety, especially with faster e-bikes and scooters, will be important to track and, probably, legislate on.

Regarding transit, I did a quick estimate for Project Connect back in 2020. My numbers were rough, but I found that bus rapid transit would be half the cost of light rail per passenger-mile, with only slightly worse performance. Since ATP did not lock in interest rates when they were low, that cost difference has risen. I don't know the exact state of Project Connect - I know contracts have gone out but I don't know their terms - but we should re-evaluate light rail with the expectation of having to cancel it and devote the money to bus rapid transit. Obviously, we should allow a lot more housing near transit (transit-oriented development).

As for cars, they make up most of our trips. The latest data seems to be from 2019, where cars made up 85% of commutes. Polls show that Austinites hate congestion and they should: according to the INRIX 2025 Global Traffic Scorecard, it costs the metro area $807 million per year. And congestion is expected to get worse with autonomous vehicles. No one benefits from a doctor sitting in traffic - we need to get Austin's roads moving again. Singapore solved congestion on all its highways over 25 years ago with its Electronic Road Pricing system. We have a small version of it here: MoPaC Express Lane, which keeps traffic moving at all hours by adjusting its tolls. So, solving the problem is possible! Obviously, no one likes the tolls on MoPaC Express Lane, so Austin must innovate to find a new way that is popular with voters. Maybe we only apply it to autonomous vehicles. Maybe we give every resident an equal number of tokens to use for tolls, instead of dollars. Maybe we have drivers schedule when they do their commute. There are many possible ways to do it - we just need to find one that works and that people will accept. I will propose that Austin fund research to create and evaluate these ideas. Kara Kockelman, a professor at UT, is a local expert who can help the City select the researcher. If there is a solution people accept and we build it, that infrastructure will dramatically change Austin's housing demand, as highways carry more cars during rush hour and carry them more quickly to their destination.

As for utility infrastructure, I have read a little about the subject but do not have specific plans. We should consider congestion pricing and tradable rights for pipes and wires that are near capacity limits. Since prices for electricity fluctuate, we should force companies to pay market prices and allow residential customers to switch to them. We should look at financial and physical ways to deal with the unreliability in the state's energy transmission grid, which scares both residential and commercial customers.

Q: What additional land use or regulatory reforms would you support to increase the supply and diversity of housing citywide?

I would dramatically shrink the minimum lot size and increase the units-per-acre. I would eliminate limits on volume (floor-to-area ratio). I would raise height limits. I would allow housing in almost every location, except where it is dangerous (e.g., industrial zones).

I would allow manufactured housing on any lot, but preserve neighborhood appearance by allowing neighborhoods to create a menu of manufactured housing that would be welcome in their area. If they do not create a menu with sufficient choices, any manufactured house would be allowed.

Q: How can the City of Austin promote the creation of workforce housing that is geographically distributed across the city and the metro area?

One limitation to location is price. As I've measured, land prices go up exponentially as you get closer to downtown. The solution here is to allow a smaller minimum lot size and more units per acre. By paying for less land for each unit, workers can afford to live closer to all the jobs downtown.

Another limitation is deed restrictions. These contain some limits that made sense at the time (and some that never made sense!) but are unsuitable now. Many deed restrictions have a minimum lot size or units-per-acre restriction. Austin needs to work with the state to enact a time limit on deed restrictions. As part of eliminating zoning restrictions, I would offer to enforce new deed restrictions that contained a time limit and incentivize land owners to remove old deed restrictions.

Q: How should Council structure density bonus and incentive programs to deliver affordability without stalling housing production?

Affordability is best delivered by fixing the housing shortage. That means more housing at all price levels, especially the lowest. This means removing laws that force a minimum price in an area, like the minimum lot size, which requires buying a minimum amount of expensive land.

So-called "inclusionary zoning" requires more expense, more paperwork, and more time by the builder. It is a tax on housing and increases prices. Housing vouchers are a better solution. If landlords are not accepting vouchers in an area, we should talk to landlords about the issues and fix those. At worst, the City can pay landlords to accept vouchers, by holding a reverse auction where landlords with the lowest bids to accept vouchers get paid.

Q: How should preservation bonuses be used to retain existing housing while enabling new units?

We do not need to provide a bonus to keep existing housing. Existing housing is already cheaper because the owner doesn't have to pay for demolishing the existing building and building a new one.

Q: What specific actions should Council take to improve predictability and decrease costs in the residential permitting and site plan review process, including Site Plan Lite and residential infill subdivision?

I like the steps that the City has taken, like Site Plan Lite, that realize small infill projects are different from building a subdivision with hundreds of homes. I would push for specific clear checklists, so developers know what to expect and reviewers can quickly assess requirements. Fees should be predictable. For ADUs and some other projects, the City can keep a set of pre-approved plans that can skip some steps. The City should be able to give applicants a definitive time window. Unexpected delays will happen and we should explore whether the City can defray the applicant's costs by refunding fees or providing insurance against delays.

Q: How will you hold the City Manager accountable for departmental service levels and transparent performance metrics related to the development review process?

In a council-manager government, the City Manager has a lot of power. So, I would start by motivating the City Manager. Improving development can bring in more money without raising taxes. Speeding up development will improve the City's budget and avoid problems and difficult choices in the future.

As for accountability, I would start by setting metrics. I worked at Motorola and they taught me that the design of metrics is important: they should be clear, within the City Manager's control, gathered frequently, difficult to game, and have a clear direction (e.g., lower is better). After having good public metrics, I would set achievable targets for the metrics (e.g., 50% reduction by next year) and schedule regular public discussions of progress. This will include the City Manager's performance evaluation.

Because of my skills in data science and econometrics, I can analyze raw department data to detect manipulation or circumvention of the metrics.

Q: How will you balance the City budget while minimizing impacts on homeowners and renters, and ensuring efficient use of taxpayer resources?

My background is in finance and economics, so I've given this some thought.

First, we need good cost-benefit analysis. I did my own estimates for Project Connect in 2020 and they cast doubt on the light-rail plan back then and, depending on the state of contracts, cost-benefit analysis may conclude that the light-rail should be replaced with bus rapid transit. The financial report for the convention center was woefully inadequate and should not have been used in the decision to demolish the old one and build a new one. The City does a lot of surveys of residents, but I've never seen a question of "How much do you value this?" or "What would you do without this?". Without knowing residents' alternatives, we don't know their costs or benefits and can't make good decisions. So, part of this is asking residents the right questions.

Second, we need to fix the underfunded pensions. That cost the City $219 million per year. (Search for "Interest" in the pension reports.) There are three parts to this. (1) Shift the debt to cheaper fixed-rate bonds, when we can. The lowest rate GO bonds need to be backed by infrastructure, so it may require budget manipulations and discipline. (2) Target the budget to the low end of predicted revenue and devote any excess revenue to funding the pension. (3) Grow the population. More people means less debt-per-person.

Third, we should prioritize development that has a high revenue-to-cost ratio. Consultants, like Urban3, have done revenue modeling for other cities. Those studies showed that some development brings in more money for the city than it costs the city. We should prioritize building this. (It is often multi-family buildings near downtown.)

Q: What is your approach to development review fees, utility infrastructure costs, and impact fees as they relate to housing affordability?

I have read "Regulation for Revenue", which is a book about impact fees. Its conclusion was that they are unpleasant, but they are politically viable and not the worst solution. I'd prefer for homeowners to pay for the City's costs through property taxes, not impact fees. But because impact fees are seen as falling on new people moving to Austin, I don't know whether the change will be politically possible.

Q: Would you support or oppose any form of rental regulation, including rent control measures, rent stabilization laws, or a mandatory, blanket rental registration program for Austin? Why or why not?

I would support rent limits only in an emergency and only for a temporary amount of time. Economists have studied price limits on various necessities and they almost always end badly. When there is a housing shortage, rent control/stabilization reduces the incentive to produce new housing that will fix the shortage.

It is better to provide financial assistance directly to the people affected. This is what housing vouchers try to do. We need to have more of those for the worst off and get them accepted at more locations. That is a much better fix than rent limits.

Q: Would you support or oppose any additional rental regulations, including renters’ rights laws or ordinances that add additional requirements to rental housing providers? Why or why not?

I support a legal defense fund for renters. It is relatively low cost and would encourage landlords to keep the commitments they make.

I know windowless bedrooms are an issue, especially near UT. City Council banned new ones, but some already exist. I would allow someone renting a windowless bedroom to end the lease early with a note from a doctor confirming circadian rhythm problems. I think this will incentivize owners to rent these units to the 6% of people who work night shifts and who benefit from a windowless bedroom.

Q: What is your prior professional or community engagement with REALTORS® in the greater Austin area?

I considered becoming a realtor, in order to do house price analysis for a current realtor. Texas law requires it for the work.

Q: If the ABoR decides to support your campaign, would you accept a contribution from our Austin Board of REALTORS® Political Action Committee (ABoRPAC)?

No.

Austin Area Urban League

2026-08-06 · Questionnaire

Q: Please write a brief statement of you position on Economic Mobility & Small Business Support.

Being able to start your own business provides economic safety. It provides income when there are not jobs available. And having the option to create your own business provides negotiating power when discussing salary with an employer. The City of Austin should make it very easy to start your own business. We've done that with food trucks and it has benefitted both workers and customers. The City should expand the locations where businesses can be run and foster markets where micro-businesses can meet customers.

Economic mobility also comes from increasing education and skills. City should organize and buy online educational resources for residents. City should fund jobs/skills education with positive externalities, like learning English. The City should make loans available on friendly terms for jobs/skills education with positive return-on-investments.

Q: Please write a brief statement of your position on Criminal Justice and Public Safety.

Austin has about a million people and most of them are strangers to us. We want to feel safe and we want the power we entrust to the government to do that be used fairly. I'm a data person and we should measure safety not by using numbers generated by the police, like number of arrests, but external measures such as the cost of insurance and surveys of resident safety. We need to measure racial inequities in both the Austin's police actions and the Travis County court system and, when found, fix them. There reasons to believe they exist: Census Tract 23.19 contains the Travis County Correctional Complex and, in the 2020 Census, 29% of people identified as "Black or African American only". That was the 5th highest of the 289 tracts in Travis County.

We should continue to expand using specialists for responses to emergency calls, such as for mental health emergencies. For EMS, we need to make sure EMS can be hired and make a living in Austin. For Firefighters, we need to keep an eye on response times. Traffic congestion plays a part, which is an issue that I consider important for all Austinites.

Q: Please write a brief statement of your position on Public Health Disparities and Health Equity that impact District 1.

The first step will be to make sure a goal of City government is to lengthen average life span. That seems obvious, but the number is not tracked on Austin's ePerformance website. With a goal of longer average life span, District 1 benefits because lower-income and Black residents have shorter expected life spans and it is easier to lengthen a short expected life span than a longer one.

Public Health is always multi-pronged. The usual methods are health tracking, preventive healthcare (at the forthcoming Colony Park Health & Wellness Center), exercise, weight control, nutrition, and stopping smoking. We should vigorously pursue Vision Zero to lower traffic deaths. And work to prevent suicides and overdose deaths, which also cut lives short. We should explore buying GLP-1s in bulk at a low price for the 50% of our population that is overweight. We should explore social programs to fight loneliness. Also, better healthcare for 0-4 year olds has a high return on investment, because their whole lives are in front of them.

Q: Please write a brief statement of your position on Education & Youth Development for District 1.

I want to foster youth sports, such as Ismael Guzman's East Austin Soccer Club and, if possible, restarting Austin Midnight Basketball. These provide teens with exercise, connection, learning to work as a group, and opportunities for leadership and to excel.

A large research study showed 2 big govt policies that gave great returns on investment: education of 0-4 year olds and helping young high school graduates, who wouldn't normally go to college, get there. Where I can, I'll encourage policies in these areas.

Q: Please write a brief statement of your position on Housing and Affordability for District 1.

I've worked since 2017 to lower the rents in Austin. I and other economists supported laws that encouraged more housing and decreased the minimum lot size. City Council passed these laws and the Pew Foundation has credited them with lowering the rents by $250 per month since the peak during covid. That's $3,000 per year for poor Austinites. I will continue to pursue laws that encourage lower rents and cheaper starter homes. A smaller minimum lot size will put homeownership in reach of more people and allow more Black families to afford to live closer to cultural institutions, like Carver Library and Downs Field.

We should encourage families to get SNAP, which will both feed the poor and bring Federal money into our local economy.

Q: Please write a brief statement of your position on Inclement Weather Response for District 1.

Weather is the most likely kind of emergency in Austin: heat, ice, flood, wind, hail, hurricanes and tornadoes. The first line of defense is neighbors, who are close by to provide support. We need supplies stashed at libraries and rec centers nearby, people who know they're there, and how to get to them in an emergency without power or communication. (City of Austin has the "Austin Resilience Network", which could be this, but online documentation is lacking.) Top priorities are water, power, and communication. Given ERCOT's ability to take Austin's power and send it elsewhere in an emergency, we need to continue pursuing dynamic rates with commercial customers so during a shortage residential power can be preserved. We also need to innovate: during the last freeze, I and a colleague sent mass texts to residents of apartment complexes to identify where the water wasn't working and get water delivered by the Austin Firefighters Association.

Q: Please write a brief statement of your position on Police Oversight and Accountability.

First, Austin Police Oversight should be under the City Auditor, not under the City Manager. The City Manager negotiates the contract with the Austin Police Association and we do not want oversight to be even potentially part of that negotiation. The City Auditor is the correct place for all oversight.

Otherwise, accountability is difficult. The existing police contract gives the police rights that most defendants do not get and a lengthy appeals process, with many off-ramps in their favor. And the police can reject any new contract until 2029. I have ideas for future contracts, including that the police must be responsible for settlements caused by their mistakes, such as $35 million to the 4 wrongly accused of the yogurt shop murders. The union can buy insurance for it and, when they do, the insurance company will tell them that the premium or deductible is higher because of certain riskier members of the force. They will have the incentive to see riskier members in less powerful positions and hold their own accountable.

Q: Please write a brief statement of your position on City of Austin need for bureaucratic efficiency waste reduction.

I spent 100 hours last year looking in the City's pension systems. The City has borrowed $3.1 billion from the pension funds at a high interest rate. The interest is $219 million, equal to 1/7 of the General Fund! A top priority will be reducing that waste through (1) moving debt to lower interest rates, (2) prioritizing development with high tax revenue relative to cost, (3) moderate population growth, and (4) devoting any higher-than-expected income to pay down the debt.

Our planning department still needs dramatic improvement. The 2023 McKinsey report said site plans took 345 days and people requesting them gave the City a 2.8 out of 10. Recently, I talked to a religious organization that wanted to build a gym and they have been at it for over 3 years! High interest rates have slowed new projects, so we should work on training, clarity, consistency, simplification, and making common requests fast.

Q: Please write a brief statement of your position on equitable Workforce & Career Development opportunities for the City of Austin.

The City of Austin should continue to be an example employer, such as for those with criminal histories. As I mentioned in a previous answer, the City should provide online materials, fund education with positive externalities (like teaching English), and provide loans for education with positive return-on-investments. From an equity perspective, funding should prioritize the largest percentage increase in salary, which will send most resources to people with lower incomes (which are easier to increase by a large percentage).

Austin Sierra Club

2026-08-19 · Questionnaire

Q: What do you see as the biggest environmental concern in your district? What about in the city as a whole?

I think the biggest environmental concern in District 1 and the city is pollution. As an economist, I try to focus on people's incentives and one of the biggest problems is externalities, where a person's actions affect other people but they never pay the cost of it. Pollution can be an awful externality, affecting air quality, water quality, and soil quality. As a society, we've tried to internalize pollution, which means making polluters pay the cost. They must pay to clean their waste or pay fines for violating the law. But that requires constant wide-ranging monitoring and strict enforcement to deter cheaters. It also requires precise crafting of laws so that we keep the environment pure without overly disincentivizing business and innovation.

Relative to other cities, Austin has mostly done well with pollution. We never used our rivers for shipping and our major early industries (state government and UT-Austin) were clean. But land contamination and dirty power plants did happen, often on the east side.

The City should internalize the cost of carbon ("carbon tax") for itself and its suppliers. And promote that idea to major industries in Austin. The City needs to monitor new industries, like manufacturing, which use toxic substances. It should monitor existing industries that have pollution risks, such as the Decker Creek Power Station and the dump at 9900 Giles Ln.. It should monitor air quality, both outdoors and indoors, especially east of the wider I-35. The City should work with the Barton Springs-Edwards Aquifer Conservation District and other local governments to monitor pollution with the extended and expanded MoPac and preserve Barton Springs. It should track health outcomes that might be tied to pollution, like asthma or poisoning.

Q: City Council took a secret vote on gas peaker plants. What importance do you place on increasing public transparency when it comes to major decisions that have long term environmental and financial implications?

I am disgusted by the City Council Members' actions. It was a grave violation of democracy. We need to know our representatives' actions so that we can vote appropriately.

I would never do what they did and hide how I voted.

We should examine modifying the City Charter to prevent it in the future. State law may supersede our Charter. I agree with the goal of the state law that some specific values, like prices, need to remain hidden during negotiations. But that needs to be temporary and kept to an absolute minimum. I would need to talk to a lawyer to see if Austin's Charter could be modified to prevent this in the future. If it can, I would heartily support that change.

Q: Regarding Dog's Head developement, what issues, if any, do you see with fast-tracking approval of an agreement of this size and scope via a process without the public transparency which the community has come to expect? What do you see as the most important priorities going forward as the city continues to navigate the development of this site?

I would have voted against Dog's Head for economic reasons: it is far from the city's center and, in the long term, unlikely to have tax revenue that exceeds its costs. The modified approval process appalled me because many employees of the developer, Endeavor Real Estate Group, made donations to City Council Members in a short period of time (see below). Laws should apply the same to everyone and that propriety is even more important to maintain when political donations are involved. (NOTE: I have not accepted donations from anyone. I am running this campaign on my own funds.)

My priorities are the same as any development. Dog's Head is low and close to the river, so, in this case, flooding and pollution are especially important. Transportation is also a special issue, since the river surrounds the property and bridges are expensive to build.

Our residents should have the freedom to arrange the inside of their homes however they want. Be it townhomes, triplexes, duplexes, or single-family homes.

Q: Would you have voted for the HOME initiative as passed? Should HOME projects be easier to build, or should there be additional restrictions and requirements? What changes would you make to HOME, if any?

Yes, I would have voted for HOME phase 1 and 2 as passed. They were a dramatic improvement on previous laws.

I would change HOME to eliminate the minimum lot size and maximum units-per-lot. Especially within 3 miles of downtown, the Capitol, and UT, where residents could walk, bike/ebike/scooter, and ride transit. The minimum lot size and maximum units-per-lot force buyers to purchase more expensive land than they want and drive up the cost of owning or renting a house.

HOME made it easier to put more units on a lot, but didn't make it easier to split a lot. Even with later ordinances, it is still cheaper and easier for developers to create "horizontal condos" rather than split lots. I would make it easier to split a lot and incentivize undoing of horizontal condo agreements. The condo agreements are an unnecessary complication that affects insurance, inheritance, lawsuits, tax filing, and future development. These agreements are not as bad as deed restrictions, but I expect they will last just as long. The City of Austin needs to make lot splitting the default and incentivize owners of horizontal condos to convert them to split lots. We should do it now, when there are few, rather than later.

Q: What role has building more market-rate housing played in the rent reductions Austin has seen in the past few years? What effect do you believe building market-rate housing has on affordability and displacement?

More market-rate housing has played a big part in lowering rents. Zillow says metro Austin's rent has fallen 11% in 4 years, the best in the country. Pew agrees.

https://www.zillow.com/research/data/
https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2026/03/18/austins-surge-of-new-housing-construction-drove-down-rents

More market-rate housing has lowered the cost of existing houses. This graph shows the nominal price staying stable, which means the inflation-adjusted price has fallen about 5.7% since HOME passed.

https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/ATNHPIUS12420Q

Thus, the effect of more market-rate housing on affordability is clear.

The effect on displacement is more complicated. When discussing displacement, it is important to keep in mind that it happens to a community, not an individual. Members of the community will always be leaving their homes. Some will move to different cities or pass away. It is important to check if younger members of the same community are moving into the neighborhood. If that stops, displacement is sure to happen.

Let's consider displacement for renters and owners, when market restrictions have been removed. That is, redevelopment is more profitable and older buildings are more likely to be sold, torn down, and replaced by more new housing.

If the residents of the older building rented, they may look to rent nearby. Because many buildings in a neighborhood were built at the same time, they will get redeveloped at the same time. The neighborhood will have fewer and fewer old buildings and more and more new buildings. The new buildings are more expensive because they are new and have nicer features (new appliances, modern styling, new technology, etc.). If they remain renters, they will usually choose to find a new place in a different neighborhood. This is because there are usually older units for rent elsewhere in the city that are similar to their previous unit in price, size, and amenities. The market could create new units with the same rent in the same neighborhood, but they will be smaller and/or have fewer features. Other members of their community are unlikely to move into the neighborhood, for the same reasons. Thus, increased market-rate housing is likely to accelerate displacement of people who remain renters in the area.

If the residents of the older building owned it, then they chose to sell it at that time. Let's focus on those that had a life event that required moving elsewhere, such as to be near grandchildren or into a retirement community. So, they will not want to live in one of the new buildings; will a younger member of their community want to? Possibly. It will depend on the price. The communities suffering displacement have fewer individuals with wealth and/or credit to buy expensive houses. So, the determining factor for owner displacement is the price of the new units.

The lower bound for house price is determined by the minimum lot size. Land in historic east Austin costs over $50 per square foot. So, requiring 5,750 sqft meant the price for the land to build a house is over $287,500. What can a Black household afford? The median Black household income in the US is $55,000. If they spent the maximum recommended on a mortgage, taxes, and insurance (28%) at current mortgage rates (6.71%), they might afford a house with a total cost of $191,000. Since the land is at least $287,500, the median Black household cannot even afford the land with a 5,750 sqft minimum lot size.

Our new minimum lot size of 1,800 sqft makes the land cost at least $90,000. If we assume it is exactly $90,000, the median Black household can afford $101,000 for the building, which is possible with a manufactured home. So, the smaller minimum lot size means the median Black household can afford to buy in their historic community. But more than just the median should live there and it is likely that demand will bid up the price of those small lots. I would get rid of the minimum lot size entirely. There should not be a minimum wealth necessary to own land in historic east Austin.

Thus, removing market restrictions for a neighborhood with a low-income community is likely to increase displacement among people who remain renters and decrease displacement among new homeowners. That sounds bad for renters, but consider what those restrictions also did: they kept older buildings around. They delayed the construction of new buildings that have new appliances, modern styling, and new technology, including energy efficient insulation and heat pumps. Those laws didn't stop redevelopment - new buildings would get built eventually. The laws kept residents in older, crappier buildings longer than necessary. The members of the community that remain renters benefit from "filtering": with fewer old buildings, their new home is likely to be newer built than their old one and have more amenities. I know this does not compensate for the negative effects of displacement on the communities, such as being farther from cultural institutions and making it more difficult to organize to protect their communities. But I don't see how those are avoidable, without extreme subsidies or condemning Austin's residents to live in older and older buildings. The best of the realistic options is more market-rate housing with the opportunity that more of the community can afford to buy homes in their historic parts of Austin.

Q: Do you support increasing the number of people that choose to walk, bike, or take transit to meet their daily needs in your district, and if so, what changes are both necessary and achievable to achieve that goal?

Yes.

For walkers, we need wide sidewalks downtown and, elsewhere, we need sidewalks where they walk. I've seen a blind kid walking in the street because there were no sidewalks! We especially need sidewalks near transit infrastructure. On City Council, I will devote more money to sidewalks.

For cyclists, we need bike lanes in the right places. I've seen good bike lanes, like on 3rd and 4th streets downtown, which have bikes on them at all hours. And I've seen bad bike lanes, like on 183 Frontage Rd near my house. The speed limit is 55 mph! And the bike lane isn't protected - it is just a line on the ground.

As a rule of thumb for "good bike lanes", I think they are within 3 miles of our major destinations: downtown, the Capitol, and UT. The Dutch Ministry of Infrastructure and Water Management data indicates that shorter trips are more likely to be taken by bike or e-bike. For distances between .5 kilometers and 5 kilometers, more than a third of all trips are by bike. For distances larger than 5 kilometers, it drops off. 5 kilometers is 3.11 miles, which I round to 3. Within 3 miles of downtown, the Capitol, or UT, I will work to get a protected bike lane on every 3rd street.

https://english.kimnet.nl/site/binaries/site-content/collections/documents/2024/01/10/cycling-facts-2023/KiM+brochure+Cycling+facts+2023_def.pdf

For transit riders, it will depend on housing. Transit works best with dense housing near downtown. When I talked to voters, I got requests for bus service far from downtown, because that is where lower-income households are. But it would not be cost effective, because the housing is not dense and it is far from downtown. I will work for transit-oriented development that creates the density that supports transit. I will work to expand high-speed bus lines. I will push for shorter times between bus arrivals, so that wait time is minimized. And I will push for short times to board transit.

My priorities for City Council are to [1] increase the disposable income (income adjusted for cost of living) of Austinites, [2] increase Austinites' value of City services, and [3] increase Austinites' life spans. For sidewalks, bike lanes, and trails, they can increase Austinites' value of City services. Sidewalks and protected bike lanes are not only safer but more pleasant to travel on. Q: Given the harsh fiscal and economic realities surrounding the City of Austin, what would be your strategy for funding or producing subsidized affordable housing?

Funding should come directly from taxes. Density bonuses make it harder to build housing than just allowing the zoning by right. And "harder to build housing" is the opposite of what Austin needs.

We should limit subsidies to housing for the poor. That includes Permanent Supportive Housing (PSH) for those unable to run their own lives and vouchers for households under 30% MFI.

Current Austin housing policy can subsidize housing at the 65% or even 80% MFI level. MFI is called "Median Family Income" but the formula derives from the median 4-person family. The median 4-person family has 2 incomes. So, 100% MFI is roughly equal to twice the median individual income. Thus, with 65% MFI, more than half of single-income families qualify and at 80%, I expect, more than half of all families qualify. While I sympathize with their need, I believe those families are best served by lowering the cost of market-rate housing. I will redirect subsidies to those whose need is highest at the 30% MFI level.

Q: What do you believe are the barriers to building more housing in all parts of Austin, especially high-resource areas, and how would you work to reduce these barriers?

There are a number of barriers: zoning, deed restrictions, transportation, political discretion, and market inertia.

Zoning restricts housing by banning it. First, I'll make our zones hierarchical like in Europe and Japan: allowing residential buildings in every zone except industrial. Second, I'll loosen the limits on height, on volume (floor-area ratio or FAR), setbacks, and units-per-acre (including the minimum lot size). The units-per-acre is the most important because it forces families to buy a minimum amount of expensive land, driving up the minimum cost to own a home. There shouldn't be a minimum cost to own in Austin.

Deed restrictions are contracts attached to land that can restrict anything: number of units, setbacks, and, when courts enforced them, the race of the owner. The biggest current problem with deed restrictions is that they can last forever. We limit most contracts to 99 years - there should be a limit for deed restrictions. I will ask the state to put a time limit on deed restrictions, unless renewed by all parties. I will offer City enforcement of deed restrictions that have a 10-year limit, as long as there are no other deed restrictions on the property. I hope this will encourage owners to organize to remove old deed restrictions, especially those with the disgusting racial clauses. No buyer should have to agree to a racist clause, even depowered, to complete the purchase of a home.

Transportation is important because residents include transportation in their decision of where to live. We cannot just have affordable housing - it needs to be paired with affordable transportation. That means walking, bike/ebike/scooter, and transit. I will push for wider sidewalks downtown. I will push for a protected bike lane on every 3rd street within 3 miles of downtown, the Capitol, and UT. I will push for expanding high-speed buses and buses having rapid arrivals. If it is still cost-effective, I will support light rail.

Political discretion is requiring a political vote to build housing or, more usually, change the zoning to allow housing to be built. It restricts housing by making the process uncertain and making it harder to build unpopular housing, even if it is needed. My favorite example of unpopular housing is Community First Village, which is a wonderful development for the formerly unhoused, but was unable to be built in Austin because of political discretion. If something is unfit for Austin, we should have a clear law about it and let judges decide, not politicians. This is best solved by expanding zoning, to avoid the discretionary zoning changes, and making the laws by-right not by-City-Council-vote.

Lastly, market inertia is when new builders do not enter the market and the builders in the market don't try new buildings. We can encourage new builders to enter the market by simplifying and standardizing our laws. This will let builders from other areas in Texas more easily build in Austin and that competition will lower prices for our residents. As for new building types, we should encourage the building of Single Room Occupancies (SROs). These are dorms for adults: small rooms where residents share bathrooms and share kitchens. These have the chance to be extremely affordable market-rate housing that would be available to our residents who are currently temporarily homeless. Austin has not built many SROs and, even if zoning allows them, we may need to offer grants to builders to take the risk to build a new type of building and start the business to operate it.

Q: If you were on Council today, would you vote to support the City's current MoPac South opposition efforts (including comments to CAMPO, CTRMA, and TxDOT)? Should the City take a stronger stance against the expansion, a more accommodating one, or maintain its current position? What changes to the project, if any, would you push for?

Going from City Council Resolution 20260423-064, I agree with some aspects of the City's position and disagree with some.

I agree with its recommendations to "provide continuous, safe pedestrian and bicycle trails and crossings" and "support express transit service". Austin should promote green high-capacity transportation.

I agree with the purpose but disagree with the wording of their recommendation to "reduce or not increase vehicle miles traveled". Cities are for people to meet with other people. More traveling means meeting more people - it literally fulfills the purpose of a city. Yes, there are negative consequences for travel by car: collision deaths, collision costs, pollution, noise, land use, time, etc.. But there are negative consequences for every form of travel. Austin should make sure residents pay for the negative consequences of their choices - this is known in economics as a Pigouvian tax - but not try to limit their choice of travel in any way. I'd prefer this recommendation be worded to prevent the negative consequences associated with car travel rather than limit vehicle miles traveled.

I also disagree in part with the recommendation to "add no more than one lane in each direction". I am not in favor of widening the entire highway, but I am in favor of expanding the bridge dramatically. The Colorado River cuts through Austin, separating downtown from nearby land south of the river. As the City grows, we need more connections across the river. I'd prefer high-capacity green ones, with bus lanes and bike lanes, but I accept that many residents use cars. I know that expanding the bridge will not dramatically reduce congestion - within 3 years, induced demand will kick in. People who drive more often will move south of the river or people already there will drive more often. But a wider bridge will increase the number of trips taken, which is a benefit in itself. Cities are for people to meet with other people and a wider bridge will increase meetings.

Lastly, I agree with the recommendation that the project "incorporates targeted modifications designed to alleviate known problem areas and improve overall traffic flow". This is just common sense.

Q: What best describes your view of the light rail system approved by voters in 2020? (Assume it will survive legal challenges and will receive matching funds from the federal government.)

When light rail was proposed, I did a rough cost-benefit analysis. I found that light rail was better than the (total, unsubsidized) cost of a car, but bus-rapid-transit (BRT) was better than both. The cost-per-passenger-mile for BRT was about half that of light rail. Since light rail was better than a car, I did not oppose it. But, when Project Connect was proposed, there were low interest rates. Interest rates have risen and ATP did not lock in the lower rates. I know this, because the plans have shrunk. I now suspect that cost-per-passenger-mile for rail is higher than the (total, unsubsidized) cost of a car. Depending on the state of the contracts, we should change from light rail to bus rapid transit.

Q: What will you do to address challenges with mental health and substance abuse and their effects on public order and safety?

We should start by providing personalized help, such as with the new No Wrong Door initiative. This is both helpful and can control costs. For substance abuse, our help should include medications that reduce cravings and withdrawal for addictive drugs. For the worst affected, who have difficulty running their lives, we need permanent supportive housing (PSH), which puts care staff nearby. For untreated mental health, we also need to expand involuntary commitment authority to doctors and fund step-down care. Lastly, we need to expand public safety staffing with responders trained for mental health and substance abuse events.
